What Cyber Insurance Does Not Cover: The Exclusions That Surprise Claimants

Mondelez lost $100 million to NotPetya and Zurich denied the claim. Merck won in court — then Lloyd's rewrote the clause. War exclusions, vendor-outage carve-outs, and application warranties decide more claims than any attack does.

Prime Domain Capital  ·  November 2026

In June 2017, the information technology staff at Mondelez International watched a piece of malware called NotPetya move through their network faster than anyone could pull the plugs. Within hours it had encrypted systems across the food maker's global operations. The damage ran to about $100 million. Mondelez filed a claim. Its insurer, Zurich, denied it — on the ground that the attack was a hostile or warlike action, excluded from the policy. A company that made biscuits and chocolate found itself litigating the law of war.

Mondelez settled with Zurich in 2022, on undisclosed terms, and the question was never answered in court. That is the pattern with cyber insurance exclusions: the policyholder discovers what the policy does not cover at the worst possible moment, and the answer usually arrives as a settlement, not a ruling. The exclusions that matter most — war, infrastructure failure, prior knowledge — are the ones no buyer reads until the claim is already filed.

War, rewritten

The war exclusion is the most litigated clause in cyber insurance, and NotPetya wrote its modern history. The 2017 attack, attributed by Western intelligence agencies to the Russian military, hit Merck, Mondelez, and the shipping giant Maersk. Merck filed a claim of about $1.4 billion under its property program. Its insurers invoked the hostile and warlike action exclusion. On May 1, 2023, the Superior Court of New Jersey Appellate Division ruled the exclusion did not apply: the language contemplated traditional military action, not an attack on a non-military company's computer systems. The parties settled in January 2024, after years of dispute, with the policyholder holding the only appellate ruling on the question.

The market's response was to rewrite the clause rather than accept the ruling. In November 2021 the Lloyd's Market Association published model exclusions for state-backed cyberattacks, and Lloyd's required its managing agents to attach a state-backed cyberattack exclusion to standalone cyber policies starting in 2023. The market has since standardized the LMA5567A and LMA5567B clause forms, which draw the line differently from the old war wording: some versions exclude losses from state-backed operations outright, others write back coverage for attacks on the policyholder that are not part of a war. The critical fact for a buyer is that no appellate court has yet tested the new wording at scale. Merck won under the old language. A policy bought today probably does not contain the old language — and the new language was drafted by the side that lost.

When nothing was hacked

The second surprise is that a cyber policy may not respond when nothing was attacked at all. On July 19, 2024, a faulty software update from CrowdStrike crashed about 8.5 million Windows devices worldwide. Airlines, hospitals, and banks went dark. No hacker was involved. Parametrix, the risk-modeling firm, put direct financial losses to Fortune 500 companies at $5.4 billion, excluding Microsoft — and insured losses at just $540 million to $1.08 billion, roughly 10 to 20 percent of the economic damage. Policy limits, retentions, waiting periods, and coverage exclusions absorbed the difference.

Delta Air Lines carried the sharpest end of it. The carrier canceled more than 5,400 flights as its crew-tracking systems failed to recover, and later pursued CrowdStrike for losses above $500 million. The insurance question underneath was structural: many cyber policies cover system failure — a non-malicious outage of the policyholder's own systems — but treat a vendor's failure differently. Contingent or dependent business interruption cover, which responds to outages at a supplier, is often a separate grant with its own sublimit and waiting period, and cloud providers may not count as vendors at all under the wording. In January 2025 the Lloyd's Market Association published a framework for assessing exactly this aggregation risk, warning that a single shared-infrastructure failure can trigger simultaneous claims across unconnected clients. The CrowdStrike outage was the demonstration.

The application is the policy

The third surprise is the most common, and the least discussed at purchase: the application answers decide the claim. In July 2022, Travelers sued its own policyholder, International Control Services, after a ransomware attack, alleging the company's April 2022 application had claimed multi-factor authentication protected administrative access when in fact MFA existed only on its firewall. On August 26, 2022, the parties stipulated to rescission of the $1 million policy — null and void from inception, as if it had never existed. The detail worth sitting with is what did not matter: intent. Under the majority rule in U.S. jurisdictions, a misrepresentation does not need to be deliberate to justify rescission.

The same logic runs through the failure-to-maintain exclusion. In 2015, Columbia Casualty — the CNA unit — sued Cottage Health System after the hospital chain's breach exposed the records of more than 30,000 patients and produced a $4.125 million class-action settlement. The carrier sought reimbursement under a failure to follow minimum required practices exclusion, arguing the hospital had not continuously implemented the procedures and risk controls identified in its own application — including replacing factory default settings and maintaining security patches. Reed Smith, reviewing the case in June 2023, noted that no published ruling on the exclusion emerged, which is itself the lesson: the dispute never produced law, only leverage.

The pattern repeated in public in 2025, when the City of Hamilton, Ontario disclosed that its insurer had denied its claim from a February 2024 ransomware attack that took down roughly 80% of the city's network, citing multi-factor authentication that had not been fully implemented at the time of the attack. The response had by then cost about C$18.3 million. Coalition's claims data gives the scale of the problem: 82% of denied claims involved MFA that was not fully enforced across the environment. And the National Association of Insurance Commissioners' 2025 report on the 2024 market shows how often claims end in nothing: U.S.-domiciled insurers closed 38,496 cyber claims in 2024, and only 9,941 of them closed with a payment.

The sublimit surprise

Even when a claim is covered, the number on the declarations page is rarely the number that pays. Social engineering and funds transfer fraud — the most common cyber claim in America, at 58% of incidents by Coalition's count — is frequently capped at $250,000 or less, and often pays only if staff confirmed the payment change through a separate channel. Ransomware sublimits commonly run at 25% to 50% of the aggregate. The buyer who shopped on the headline limit discovers at claim time that the operative limit was a sublimit buried three endorsements deep.

Speed is the one variable the policyholder controls after the loss. At-Bay's review of its own claims found it recovered at least some of the stolen money 70% of the time when it heard within three days. Late notice is a separate denial ground in its own right — most policies require notification within 48 to 72 hours of discovery, not 48 hours after the investigation is complete. The claim that fails is often not the claim that was excluded. It is the claim that arrived late, undocumented, or under a sublimit nobody priced.

The case for the exclusions

The insurers' defense deserves a fair hearing, because without these exclusions the product may not exist. A war exclusion is what makes a cyber policy insurable at all: no carrier can price a state military campaign, and a policy that covered one would carry a premium no business would pay. The systemic-risk carve-outs are the same logic applied to vendors — a single CrowdStrike update that can generate $5.4 billion of correlated loss across hundreds of unrelated policyholders is not a risk that can be diversified inside one insurer's book. And the application warranties are the only tool a carrier has against a buyer who describes controls it does not operate.

The rebuttal is not that exclusions are illegitimate. It is that the burden of understanding them falls on the party least equipped to carry it. The buyer is a roofer, a dentist, a distributor — not a coverage lawyer — and the exclusions are drafted by the side that lost in Merck and then rewrote the clause. The market's honest answer is that coverage for state-backed attacks, system failure, and dependent business interruption is negotiable: carve-backs exist, affirmative system-failure grants exist, higher fraud sublimits exist. They exist for buyers who ask, at renewal, with a broker who reads the endorsements. They do not exist by default.

Read it before you need it

The practical work is a pre-renewal read, not a post-loss argument. Five checks cover most of the ground. First, the war wording: does the policy exclude state-backed operations, and who decides attribution — the carrier, a government, or a court? Second, system failure and dependent business interruption: does the policy respond to a vendor's outage, what is the waiting period, and do cloud providers count as vendors? Third, the application: do the answers still match reality, with dated evidence for MFA, endpoint detection, and backups — the three controls that decide most disputes? Fourth, the sublimits: what is the fraud cap, the ransomware cap, and the waiting period, against the headline limit? Fifth, the notice window: who calls the carrier, and within how many hours of discovery?

A cyber policy is a contract written for the day everything goes wrong, and it is read — really read — only on that day. The exclusions are not fine print in the decorative sense. They are the coverage. The buyer who reads them in November, with time to negotiate a carve-back, owns a different policy from the buyer who reads them in July, with a claim number already assigned.
