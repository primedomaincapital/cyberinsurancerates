Ransomware Isn’t the Claim Anymore. Email Fraud Is.

Business email compromise and funds transfer fraud now account for 58% of all cyber claims. Ransomware is a small share of claims and most of the losses. The market is pricing the wrong risk first — and the loss ratio already shows it.

Prime Domain Capital  ·  November 2026

The wire went out on a Tuesday. A controller at a mid-size distributor received an email from her chief executive — the right name, the right signature block, a plausible request to pay a vendor invoice before quarter-end. She paid $180,000. The email had not come from her chief executive. The claim that followed was routine, fast, and paid without drama: a funds transfer fraud loss, documented, verified, closed. It was also, statistically, the most normal cyber insurance claim in America in 2026. The board never heard about it. Boards hear about ransomware.

That gap between the story and the statistics is now the central fact of the cyber insurance market. The risk that dominates board decks and headlines is not the risk that fills the claims queue. The risk that fills the claims queue is an email.

The claims mix flipped

Coalition’s 2026 Cyber Claims Report, released March 5, 2026 and built on more than 100,000 policyholders across five countries, puts the numbers plainly. Business email compromise and funds transfer fraud together accounted for 58% of all cyber incidents. Funds transfer fraud alone was 27% of claims, and 71% of those started with social engineering. Overall claims frequency rose just 3% year over year to 1.54%, while average claim severity dropped 19% to $116,000 — and 64% of claims resolved at zero cost. The typical cyber claim is now a modest, familiar fraud, not a catastrophe.

The cost profile underneath is what should worry underwriters. Business email compromise averaged about $20,000 a claim. Funds transfer fraud averaged about $106,000. Ransomware averaged about $202,000 — the costliest claim type — and Coalition’s own data puts the average ransomware loss at $269,000 in 2025. Ransomware was only 9.6% of claims but drove 91% of incurred losses. Resilience’s midyear 2026 report tells the same story from a different book: ransomware was 5.8% of claims by count in the first half of 2026 but 73% of incurred losses. A market that prices for the average claim is pricing for the email. A market that prices for the loss is pricing for the ransom.

The ransomware paradox

Ransomware itself is behaving strangely. Initial ransom demands surged 47% year over year, and recorded ransomware events hit a fresh half-year record — 4,295 events in the first half of 2026, roughly 60% of the entire 2025 total of 7,179, according to RansomGraph data published in Howden’s reporting. Yet 86% of businesses refused to pay, a record refusal rate, and in the UK the share of ransomware victims who paid fell to 18% in 2026 from 54% a year earlier, according to Sophos. The attackers are asking for more, staging more attacks, and collecting from fewer victims. The business model is under more strain than the headlines suggest — and the strain is showing up as data theft attached to almost every serious attack, a second monetization path for the same intrusion.

The soft market’s logic — and its limit

All of this is happening inside a market that keeps getting cheaper. Marsh’s Global Insurance Market Index recorded a 4% global decline in cyber rates in the second quarter of 2026, the twelfth consecutive quarterly decline. Howden’s global cyber pricing index sat 27% below its mid-2022 peak at the end of 2025 — though still 103% above pre-2020 levels. In reinsurance, the softening is sharper: cyber aggregate excess-of-loss rates fell 32% at the January 1, 2026 renewal, the eleventh consecutive quarter of negative rate change, and July renewals were still down 10% to 20%. Howden Re’s Cygenesis report, published May 6, 2026, describes a fourth consecutive year of rate softening, with capacity expanding ahead of demand.

The U.S. premium figures show the same pressure. Direct gross written premium fell from $7.25 billion in 2023 to $7.08 billion in 2024, the first year-over-year decline on record, while claim counts rose about 40% to roughly 50,000. The average domestic premium per policy in force was $1,621 in 2024 across 4,368,614 policies. AM Best’s 2025 figures show U.S. cyber premium recovering to $7.5 billion, but the industry loss ratio rose 4.3 points to 53.0% — its second consecutive increase, and the first time above 50% since the ransomware spike of the COVID era. S&P Global forecasts premium increases of 15% to 20% in 2026. The soft market has a logic: capacity grew faster than demand. It also has a limit, and the loss ratio is the instrument that measures it.

The case for staying soft

The counterargument is not wishful thinking. Coalition’s severity fell 19% in a single year, frequency rose only 3%, and nearly two-thirds of claims cost the carrier nothing. Munich Re puts the global market at nearly $15 billion in 2025 growing toward $28 billion by 2030, and Swiss Re’s estimate reaches $16.6 billion in 2026 — a market still expanding even as rates fall, because more businesses are buying. A buyer in late 2026 can plausibly argue that cyber cover has rarely been more affordable, and the data supports them — for the average claim.

The rebuttal is that averages are the wrong instrument. The loss ratio is rising because the tail is getting fatter while the middle gets cheaper: a handful of ransomware and data-theft events now carry the year. Howden Re’s warning is the sentence to remember — a moderate property-catastrophe-equivalent loss could dislocate this market, because concentration is high and diversification is limited. The last hardening, from 2019 to 2022, was driven by first-party ransomware claims that settled quickly and gave fast loss visibility. The next one may be driven by claims that surface slowly, through stolen data rather than locked files.

Underwrite the inbox

For a buyer, the implication is practical rather than theoretical. The controls that prevent the most common claim are unglamorous: multi-factor authentication everywhere, payment verification that does not rely on email alone, dual approval on vendor changes, and a culture in which a controller can call the chief executive before wiring $180,000 without fear of looking slow. Those controls are also what underwriters now ask about first, because they move the frequency number that dominates the claims queue. Ransomware readiness still matters — backups, segmentation, incident response — but it is the second conversation, not the first. The first conversation is about the inbox, where 58% of the claims already live.
