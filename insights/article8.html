How Long Does a Cyber Insurance Claim Take to Pay?

A clean claim closes in 30 to 90 days. A ransomware claim with a coverage fight takes six to ten months. The stage-by-stage timeline, and the documentation that moves your claim to the front of the queue.

Prime Domain Capital  ·  November 2026

The ransom was paid on a Tuesday. The forensics firm was on site by Thursday. The claim was filed the following Monday, and the finance director asked the question every policyholder asks at exactly that moment: when do we get the money back? The answer she got was the honest one, which is also the unhelpful one: it depends. A clean cyber claim — a straightforward incident, complete documentation, no coverage dispute — closes in 30 to 90 days. A ransomware claim with a coverage question, a regulatory investigation, or a business interruption dispute takes six to ten months. The difference is not the insurer's goodwill. It is the documentation, and the documentation is the policyholder's job.

The claim process has a shape, and the shape is the same at every carrier. First notice, triage, forensic investigation, coverage determination, loss quantification, settlement. Each stage has a duration, and each duration is set by something specific. A buyer who understands the stages can shorten the timeline. A buyer who does not will discover the timeline the hard way, one delay at a time, while the costs keep coming.

The first 72 hours set the pace

The clock starts with first notice, and the first notice should happen within 24 to 48 hours of detecting the incident. Most carriers assign a claims adjuster within 24 to 48 hours for an active incident, and the good ones approve emergency response costs immediately — forensics, containment, legal counsel — because those costs are incurred whether or not the claim is ultimately paid. The policyholder's job in the first 72 hours is containment and forensic triage, which typically takes 24 to 72 hours: the forensic team images the affected systems, determines the scope of the compromise, and issues a triage note. That note is the first document the insurer reads, and it sets the tone for everything that follows. A triage note that says "ransomware, scope unknown, containment ongoing" buys the policyholder time. A triage note that says "we don't know yet" buys nothing.

The detailed forensic report — the document that actually determines coverage — normally takes one to four weeks. It answers the questions the insurer needs answered: how the attacker got in, what was accessed, what was exfiltrated, whether the policyholder's controls were what the application said they were. That last question is the one that matters most, because it is where claims are denied. A policy that was underwritten on the basis of multi-factor authentication, endpoint detection, and patched systems will be investigated against that representation, and a gap between the application and the reality is a coverage problem. The forensic report is where that gap is found, and the report takes as long as it takes because the insurer will not pay on an incomplete one.

Coverage, then quantification

Once the forensic report lands, the insurer's assessment and acceptance typically follow in two to eight weeks. This is the stage where the claim is either accepted or contested, and the contest is usually about one of three things: whether the incident is covered at all, whether the loss falls within a sub-limit, or whether the policyholder's own conduct voids coverage. The war exclusion is the most famous version of the first fight — Mondelez lost about $100 million to NotPetya in 2017, Zurich denied the claim as a hostile act, and the parties settled in 2022 on undisclosed terms without a court ever answering the question. The sub-limit fight is more common and more mundane: a policy with a $1 million limit and a $250,000 ransom sub-limit pays $250,000 on the ransom, not $1 million, and the policyholder discovers the distinction at the worst possible moment. The conduct fight is the most dangerous: a policyholder who misrepresented its controls on the application can lose the whole claim, not just the disputed portion.

If coverage is accepted, the claim moves to loss quantification, and this is where the timeline stretches. Business interruption — the largest component of most cyber claims — cannot be quantified until the business is running again, and the quantification itself is an exercise in counterfactual accounting: what would the business have earned if the incident had not happened? That question takes forensic accountants, and forensic accountants take weeks. A small, undisputed claim with a short interruption can close in 30 to 60 days. A claim with a long interruption, a regulatory investigation, or a third-party liability component takes longer — and the long end of the range is long indeed. One UK claims guide puts final settlement at up to 300 days for complex claims with regulatory or criminal enquiries, with interim payments for urgent costs arriving in 30 to 90 days. The interim payments matter: they are what keeps the business alive while the full claim is validated. A carrier that pays interim sums promptly is worth more than a carrier that pays a larger final settlement six months later.

What speeds it up, and what slows it down

Three things speed a claim up. The first is documentation kept from day one: separate accounting for every breach-related expense, from the first forensic invoice to the last notification letter, with no commingling with ordinary operating costs. Commingled costs create disputes during settlement, and disputes create months. The second is a complete first notice: the incident described accurately, the scope stated honestly, the controls described as they actually are. An insurer that discovers a discrepancy later will re-open the investigation, and a re-opened investigation starts the clock again. The third is the carrier itself. Deloitte's 2026 global insurance outlook reports that cyber claim turnaround has fallen 45 percent since 2023, driven by automated vendor ecosystems — carriers with embedded forensic and legal vendors who can certify a loss within 48 hours. One widely cited example: a ransomware claim that took 48 days with a traditional carrier took 19 days to a provisional check and 23 days to full payment with a vendor-integrated carrier. The difference was not the policy. It was the plumbing.

Three things slow a claim down, and they are the mirror image. The first is a coverage dispute, which turns a claim into a negotiation and then into a legal matter, and legal matters are measured in quarters, not weeks. The second is a regulatory investigation — a state attorney general, the SEC, or a European regulator asking questions the insurer cannot answer until the regulator is satisfied. The third is the policyholder's own delay: a claim filed late, documentation submitted piecemeal, or a forensic report that has to be redone because the first one was incomplete. The insurer's clock does not start until the documentation is complete, and the policyholder controls when that happens.

The counterargument is that the timeline is not entirely the policyholder's fault, and the carriers know it. The claim process is designed, in part, to slow payment — to give the insurer time to investigate, to negotiate, and to settle for less than the policyholder claimed. That is not a conspiracy. It is the business model, and it is disclosed in the policy. A buyer who wants a fast claim should buy from a carrier with a reputation for fast claims, and should ask, before buying, what the carrier's average time to settlement is by incident type. Most carriers will not answer that question directly. The ones that do are the ones worth buying from.

A buyer asking how long a cyber claim takes to pay should plan on 30 to 90 days for a clean claim, six to ten months for a contested one, and should treat the documentation as the real deadline. Keep separate accounts from day one. File the claim within 48 hours. Give the forensic team everything they ask for, the first time. And buy from a carrier whose vendor ecosystem can certify a loss in days rather than weeks — because the difference between a claim paid in three weeks and a claim paid in three months is not the size of the check. It is whether the business is still there to cash it.
