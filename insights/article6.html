Cyber Insurance for Small Business: What a $1 Million Policy Actually Costs

A $1 million cyber policy costs one small business $1,500 a year and another three times that. Insureon puts the median at $1,552; Vouch puts it at $2,755. Revenue, industry, and security controls explain the spread — and the cheapest quote is usually bought before the application is filed.

Prime Domain Capital  ·  November 2026

The two quotes arrived on the same Thursday in March. A commercial roofing contractor in Columbus, Ohio — fourteen employees, $3.2 million in revenue, no prior claims — had asked his broker for a $1 million cyber policy. The first carrier came back at $1,850 a year. The second came back at $5,900. Same limit, same deductible, same company, same week. The broker's explanation was that underwriting differs. The contractor's conclusion was simpler: somebody was guessing.

Neither quote was a guess. Both were the market working as designed. Cyber insurance is the fastest-growing commercial line in America, and it is also the least legible: there is no published rate card, no standard class plan, and no two carriers weighting the same facts the same way. A $1 million policy — the default purchase for a small business — costs one company $1,500 and another three times that, and both prices can be right.

What the median buyer actually pays

Start with the broadest datasets. Insureon, which prices from roughly 100,000 small-business policies sold through its marketplace, reported in its April 2026 update that its customers pay a median of $129 a month — $1,552 a year — for cyber cover, with annual premiums ranging from about $400 to more than $8,000. Forty-one percent of its customers pay less than $100 a month; another 26% pay between $100 and $200. The typical policy in that book carries a $1 million per-occurrence limit, a $1 million aggregate, and a deductible of $1,000 to $2,500.

Vouch, a broker whose book runs heavier toward venture-backed technology companies, reports a higher median: $2,755 a year overall, or about $230 a month, with companies under $1 million in revenue at a $2,082 median and companies over $10 million at $9,202. The Cyber Readiness Institute, a nonprofit focused on small-business security, puts the range for a $1 million policy at $500 to $5,000 a year. Three honest sources, three different answers — and the disagreement is itself the finding. Insureon's customers are mostly firms with fewer than five employees and under $200,000 in revenue. Vouch's are technology companies buying higher limits. The median you are quoted depends on whose median you are standing next to.

Revenue is the spine of the price. The Cyber Readiness Institute's bands run roughly $500 to $1,500 a year for firms under $1 million in revenue, $1,000 to $3,000 for firms between $1 million and $10 million, and $3,000 to $10,000 for firms between $10 million and $50 million. Vouch's medians trace the same slope at a higher level: $2,082 under $1 million, $4,081 between $1 million and $2 million, $9,202 above $10 million. Carriers price on a rate per $1,000 of revenue, which is why a premium can rise at renewal even while market rates fall — the exposure base grew, and the rate applied to a bigger number.

Industry bends the curve further. Insureon's industry pages show retail shops paying about $58 a month — $701 a year — and home-based businesses about $64 a month, while installation contractors pay the $129 median and network security firms about $130. Healthcare practices and financial services firms, holding regulated data under HIPAA and state privacy law, run several times higher: broker-published 2026 ranges put data-heavy industries at $3,000 to $8,000 or more for the same $1 million limit. The data you hold is the exposure; the regulation around it is the multiplier.

The controls that move the quote

If revenue sets the base, security controls set the discount — and the penalty. Every major carrier now underwrites off a controls questionnaire of roughly 35 to 40 questions covering identity, endpoints, backups, email, and vendors, and the answers are verified against the carrier's own external scanning. Multi-factor authentication is the first question and the most consequential: Coalition's claims data shows 82% of denied claims involved multi-factor authentication that was not fully enforced across the environment. It is not the absence of MFA that kills claims. It is the partial rollout — MFA on email but not on the VPN, on staff but not on administrators.

The controls that earn credit are specific and measurable. Coalition offers a premium credit of up to 12.5% to policyholders using its own automated detection and response product, and Chubb describes a loss-control discount of up to 10% through a preferred managed detection and response program. The evidence behind the credits is strong: Marsh McLennan's August 2025 research found each 25% increase in endpoint detection and response deployment across workstations and laptops correlated with a further 10% drop in breach likelihood, and At-Bay's 2023 claims analysis found effective backups cut ransomware claim severity by 41%. At-Bay's 2025 data adds the reason underwriters obsess over remote access: 73% of ransomware attacks began with a VPN.

The practical consequence is that the cheapest premium is often bought before the application is filed. A firm that enforces MFA everywhere, deploys endpoint detection broadly, and tests its backups walks into the quote with verifiable answers — and carriers price what they can verify. A firm that answers the questionnaire from memory, or from what it intends to deploy next quarter, pays more at binding and risks worse at claim time, when the carrier's investigator asks for proof the controls were operating on the day of the incident.

A cheap market with a rising bill

Buyers shopping in late 2026 are catching a favorable tide. Marsh reported in October that U.S. cyber rates were flat in the third quarter of 2026 after an extended stretch of declines, while global rates fell 4% — a thirteenth consecutive quarterly decrease. WTW's 2027 outlook, published October 1, 2026, puts cyber rates somewhere between a 5% decrease and a 5% increase. A small business renewing today can plausibly get more limit and broader terms than it could two years ago, for the same money or less.

The tide has an undertow. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners reported in October 2026 that claims reported to U.S. cyber insurers rose 20% in 2025, to 56,323. The industry loss ratio climbed to 53.0% in 2025, its second consecutive increase and the first time above 50% since the ransomware spike of the COVID era. S&P Global forecasts premium increases of 15% to 20% in 2026. The soft market is real, and so is the arithmetic underneath it: more claims, at higher severity, against premiums that spent three years falling. The $1,552 median is a snapshot of a market in transition, not a permanent price.

The case for buying less

The counterargument to buying a $1 million policy at all is worth taking seriously, because for many small businesses the expected loss is smaller than the premium suggests. Coalition's 2026 claims report puts average claim severity at $116,000, down 19% year over year, and 64% of claims resolved at zero cost to the carrier. A fourteen-person roofer with no customer payment data and a $3.2 million revenue line could reasonably ask whether $1,850 a year is buying protection against a risk that, in its most common form, costs $20,000 — the average business email compromise claim — and whether the money is better spent on the controls themselves.

The rebuttal is the tail. IBM's 2025 Cost of a Data Breach Report puts the average U.S. breach at a record $10.22 million, and while that figure is dominated by large enterprises, Insureon puts the average cost of a small-business data breach at $120,000 to $1.2 million — a range that swallows a $1 million limit whole at the top end. At-Bay's review of its own claims found one in three ransomware claims in 2025 triggered business interruption coverage, and those claims averaged $510,000 against $168,000 for the rest. The median claim is cheap. The claim that closes the business is not the median claim, and it is the one the policy exists for.

What to do with the quote

For a buyer holding two quotes $4,000 apart, the move is not to pick the cheaper one. It is to get a third, from a carrier whose book matches the company's profile — a tech-enabled managing general agent like Coalition or At-Bay for a firm with strong controls, a standard-market carrier for a firm still building them. Then fix the three controls that move price and decide claims — MFA on every remote and admin account, endpoint detection on every device, tested backups — before the application is submitted, not after. Then match the limit to the real exposure: a firm whose revenue stops when its systems stop needs business interruption limit, not just a breach-response sublimit.

The $1 million policy is the most bought cyber product in America, and the price of one is knowable: roughly $1,500 a year for the smallest firms, $2,000 to $4,000 through the lower middle market, $9,000 and up past $10 million in revenue — with industry and controls able to double the number or cut it by a fifth. The quote that looks wrong is usually a different carrier pricing a different risk. The work is finding out which risk is yours.
